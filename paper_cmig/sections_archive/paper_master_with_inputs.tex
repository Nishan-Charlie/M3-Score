\title{Reference cohort composition confounds distributional evaluation of
       medical image generators: a depth-resolved audit of radiology
       foundation-model features}

\author[pdn]{Sathiyamohan Nishankar}
\ead{e17230@eng.pdn.ac.lk}
\address[pdn]{Department of Computer Engineering, University of Peradeniya,
              Peradeniya, Sri Lanka}

\begin{abstract}
Distributional metrics such as the Fr\'echet Inception Distance compare a set of
generated images against a finite set of real ones. In natural-image benchmarks
the real reference is a collection of independent photographs, so its
composition is rarely examined. Medical imaging violates that assumption twice
over: adjacent slices from one patient are near-duplicates, and multi-site
cohorts carry systematic acquisition differences. We show that these two
properties, not generator quality, can dominate the reported number. On BraTS
brain MRI, the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD$^2$) between two \emph{real}
patient cohorts drawn from the same dataset reaches $0.096$, exceeding the
$0.080$ measured between a real cohort and the output of an unconditional DDPM;
a reference built by taking the first $500$ filenames in sorted order --- a
common convenience --- selects a $17$-subject block that is systematically
atypical of the cohort, and inflates the reported score by $1.9\times$ relative
to a subject-diverse reference of identical size. We propose replacing the conventional permutation
test, which asks whether real and generated samples share a distribution and
is therefore rejected in every configuration we tested, with an \emph{empirical inter-cohort reference distribution} built from
distances between disjoint real patient blocks; this reports whether a
generator differs from the reference by more than routine inter-cohort
variation, and it makes the dependence of any reported score on cohort size
explicit. Using this protocol we then audit which encoder depth of a frozen
radiology vision transformer (RadioDINO-s16) supports which evaluation task, at
all twelve blocks. Depth is strongly task-dependent and the final block is not
the best choice for any task we measure: distributional discriminability peaks
at a mid-to-late plateau (\Lb{7}--\Lb{9}) and is $22\%$ lower at \Lb{12};
near-duplicate detection improves monotonically with depth; degradation
monotonicity fails at \Lb{1}--\Lb{2}; and $k$-nearest-neighbour precision/recall
coverage fails its own mode-drop validation at \emph{every} depth, with recall
rising rather than falling as diversity is removed. For per-image novelty
scoring, the choice of scoring rule matters more than depth: a shrinkage
Mahalanobis distance reaches AUC $0.972$ where distance to the real centroid
reaches $0.846$. We release the evaluation code and report a concrete protocol:
report the number of distinct subjects behind every reference set, calibrate
against a cohort null, audit read-out depth rather than defaulting to the final block, and do not
report $k$-NN coverage in this feature space without validating it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Generative models are increasingly proposed as a response to data scarcity in
medical imaging, where privacy constraints, annotation cost and disease rarity
limit dataset size \citep{kebaili2023deep, kazerouni2023diffusion}. Whether a
synthetic dataset is fit for a downstream purpose depends on being able to
measure how far it departs from real data, and the measurement instruments in
common use were designed for a different setting. The Fr\'echet Inception
Distance (FID) \citep{heusel2017gans} and Kernel Inception Distance (KID)
\citep{binkowski2018demystifying} embed images with an ImageNet classifier and
summarise the gap between two feature distributions as a scalar. Both have been
transplanted into medical image synthesis largely unchanged.

Most methodological criticism of this transplant has focused on the
\emph{embedding}: an ImageNet backbone encodes texture statistics that need not
correspond to radiological structure, motivating replacements built on
domain-pretrained encoders \citep{dohmen2025similarity, woodland2024feature}.
That criticism is well founded, and we take a domain-pretrained encoder as our
starting point. But it leaves a second assumption unexamined. Every
distributional metric is a comparison against a \emph{finite real reference
set}, and its value is a property of that reference as much as of the generator.
In natural-image benchmarks the reference is tens of thousands of independent
photographs, so its composition is close to the population it stands for.
Medical references violate this in two specific ways:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, topsep=2pt, itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Slices are not independent samples.} A reference of $N$ axial
      slices may come from $N$ patients or from a dozen; neighbouring slices of
      one volume are near-duplicates, so the effective sample size is closer to
      the number of subjects than to $N$.
\item \textbf{Cohorts are heterogeneous.} Public collections such as BraTS
      aggregate several contributing institutions with different scanners and
      protocols. Which subjects enter the reference therefore carries a site
      signature.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent This paper asks how much of a reported metric value those two
properties account for, and finds that on BraTS they can account for more than
the generator does. Two real patient cohorts drawn from the same dataset can
differ by more, in the metric, than a real cohort differs from unconditional
DDPM output. A reference assembled by the most convenient available route ---
the first $N$ filenames in sorted order --- selects a block that is
systematically atypical of the cohort and inflates the reported score by nearly
a factor of two relative to a subject-diverse reference of the same size. Neither effect is visible in the
number itself; both are invisible in a paper that reports $N$ but not the number
of subjects behind it.

This observation has a constructive consequence. The significance machinery
usually attached to kernel two-sample tests asks whether the real and generated
samples were drawn from the same distribution. For any competent medical
generator that null is false, and in every configuration we tested the test
rejected it at overwhelming significance regardless of generator quality; we measure
permutation $z$-scores above $400$ for every generator we consider, including
one that is a different imaging modality. The comparison a practitioner needs is
different: \emph{is this generator further from the real reference than two real
patient cohorts are from each other?} That question has a natural empirical
null, obtained by measuring distances between disjoint real subject blocks. We
formalise this inter-cohort reference distribution, show that it orders
generators the permutation test cannot, and show that it makes the cohort-size dependence of
any reported score explicit.

With a defensible reference protocol in place, we then address a question that
the shift to domain-pretrained encoders raises but has not settled: a
transformer has many layers, and papers using such encoders as metric backbones
generally read the last one. We audit all twelve blocks of RadioDINO-s16
\citep{perezrad} against four evaluation tasks --- distributional
fidelity, near-duplicate (memorization) detection, mode-drop coverage, and
per-image novelty scoring --- and find that depth is strongly task-dependent and
that the final block is the best choice for none of them. We also find that one
widely used diagnostic, $k$-NN precision/recall
\citep{kynkaanniemi2019pr}, fails its own validation at every depth, in a
direction explained by neighbourhood-radius inflation.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, topsep=2pt, itemsep=2pt]
\item We quantify reference-cohort composition as a confound in distributional
      evaluation of medical generative models, and show that on BraTS
      real-versus-real cohort distance exceeds real-versus-generated distance
      (Section~\ref{sec:res_cohort}).
\item We propose an empirical inter-cohort reference distribution that
      complements the permutation test with a comparison against inter-cohort
      heterogeneity,
      and report how it scales with cohort size (Section~\ref{sec:res_null}).
\item We provide a depth-resolved audit of a radiology foundation model across
      four evaluation tasks, showing that the a-priori final-layer choice is
      suboptimal for all of them (Section~\ref{sec:res_depth}).
\item We report that $k$-NN coverage is invalid at every depth in this feature
      space, with a mechanism, and that per-image scoring is more sensitive to
      the choice of distance than to depth (Sections~\ref{sec:res_coverage}
      and~\ref{sec:res_perimage}).
\item We audit ten published medical generation papers and find that none
      reports the number of distinct subjects behind its real reference set,
      which is what makes the confound invisible in practice
      (Section~\ref{sec:res_audit}).
\item We release code implementing the protocol and all experiments.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
% =============================================================================

\paragraph{Distributional metrics and their estimators}
FID models both feature sets as Gaussians and reports the Fr\'echet distance
between them \citep{heusel2017gans}. The estimator is biased at finite sample
size, and the bias depends on $N$ in a way that makes values from different
studies incomparable \citep{chong2020effectively}. KID
\citep{binkowski2018demystifying} replaces the Gaussian assumption with an
unbiased kernel MMD$^2$ estimator \citep{gretton2012kernel}, which removes the
sample-size bias in the estimator but not the dependence on what the reference
set contains. CMMD \citep{jayasumana2024cmmd} keeps the MMD estimator and
substitutes CLIP features for Inception features. Our estimator is a standard
unbiased multi-bandwidth RBF MMD$^2$; the contribution here is not the
estimator but what it is calibrated against.

\paragraph{Domain-specific embeddings}
Several groups have argued that ImageNet features are inappropriate for medical
evaluation and proposed radiology-pretrained substitutes, including
RadImageNet-based FID variants \citep{dohmen2025similarity} and self-supervised
chest and brain encoders \citep{perezrad}. \citet{woodland2024feature}
compare feature extractors for medical FID and report that the choice changes
conclusions. The Fr\'echet Radiomic Distance \citep{konz2025frd} moves in a
different direction, replacing learned features with handcrafted radiomic
descriptors. This literature establishes that embedding choice matters; it does
not examine the reference set, which is what we address.

\paragraph{Beyond a single scalar}
Precision/recall decompositions \citep{sajjadi2018assessing,
kynkaanniemi2019pr} and $\alpha$-precision/$\beta$-recall
\citep{alaa2022faithful} separate sample quality from coverage, and
memorization audits ask whether a generator reproduces training examples
\citep{carlini2023extracting, akbar2025beware}. These diagnostics are typically
computed in the same embedding as the fidelity score and at a single, unexamined
depth. We evaluate them across depth and find that one of them does not
transfer to this feature space at all.

\paragraph{Dependence on the reference set}
The observation that a distributional score depends on what it is measured
against is not new in the natural-image literature, and we do not claim it as
ours. \citet{chong2020effectively} showed that the FID estimator is biased in
the reference sample size, and proposed extrapolating to an infinite-sample
value. Most directly, \citet{lee2026rethinking} study the \emph{geometry} of
the reference dataset, showing across six datasets that distributional density
and effective rank explain much of how FID responds as sample quality improves,
and that concentrated references produce more favourable FID trends than
dispersed ones. Their recommendation --- report distributional metrics together
with descriptors of the reference set --- is one we endorse and extend.

Our question is different in a way that matters for medical imaging, and it is
worth stating precisely which axis is new here. That literature treats the
reference as a set of independent images and asks how its geometry in feature
space affects the metric. We ask what happens when the reference is \emph{not}
a set of independent images: when it is a set of slices drawn from a much
smaller number of patients, so that its effective sample size is governed by the
subject count rather than the image count, and when which subjects are drawn
carries systematic acquisition structure. That distinction has three
consequences with no counterpart in the natural-image setting --- a real-versus-real
comparison that can exceed the real-versus-generated one, a null that shrinks
with subject diversity while the reported score barely moves, and a
subject-level construction protocol --- and it is those we contribute. The
sampling-frame question of whether the reference cohort represents the intended
deployment population is a fourth issue, which neither that literature nor this
paper addresses.

\paragraph{Dataset bias and site effects}
That multi-site medical datasets carry scanner and protocol signatures strong
enough to be recovered by a classifier is well documented
\citep{glocker2019machine, wachinger2021detect}, as is the resulting need for
subject-level rather than image-level data splits
\citep{varoquaux2022machine}. To our knowledge this literature has not been
connected to generative evaluation metrics, where the reference set plays the
role that a test split plays in discriminative work, and where the same
leakage and composition concerns therefore apply.

\section{Materials and methods}
\label{sec:methods}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Real reference (BraTS)}
We use axial slices extracted from the BraTS 2021 training collection
\citep{baid2021rsna}, giving $38{,}781$ slices from $1{,}251$ subjects
(median $31$ slices per subject) stored as $256\times256$ 8-bit PNG. Slices are
extracted from the FLAIR volume, intensity-normalised per volume to the
$[0,1]$ range after clipping at the $1$st and $99$th percentiles, and retained
where the brain mask covers at least $5\%$ of the frame. Subject identity is
recoverable from the filename, which is what makes the cohort analysis in this
paper possible; we note that a reference distributed as a flat directory of
anonymised slice images would not permit it.

\paragraph{Generated and shifted sets}
Four comparison sets of $500$ images each are used throughout:
\emph{(i)}~\textbf{DDPM}, an unconditional denoising diffusion probabilistic
model \citep{ho2020denoising} with a UNet backbone initialised from
\texttt{google/ddpm-celebahq-256} and fine-tuned on the BraTS slices for $50$
epochs at $256\times256$ (batch $16$, learning rate $10^{-5}$, fp16), sampled
with the standard $1000$-step reverse process;
\emph{(ii)}~\textbf{WDM-3D (MRI)}, axial slices from a 3D wavelet diffusion
model \citep{friedrich2024wdm} trained on BraTS, a same-modality generator of
visibly lower slice-level quality;
\emph{(iii)}~\textbf{LIDC CT}, axial slices from the same 3D architecture
trained on LIDC-IDRI chest CT \citep{armato2011lidc}, a cross-modality shift;
and \emph{(iv)}~\textbf{Retinal}, colour fundus images from RetinaMNIST
\citep{yang2023medmnist}, a far-domain shift. Sets (ii)--(iv) provide an
a-priori severity ordering against which a metric can be checked.

\paragraph{Lesion-level evaluation (LGG)}
For the lesion-specificity analysis we use the lower-grade glioma collection of
\citet{buda2019association}, which supplies expert tumour masks registered to
the slices, allowing perturbation to be confined to annotated tumour tissue or
to area-matched healthy control regions.

\subsection{Reference set construction}
\label{sec:refproto}

Let $\mathcal{S}$ be the set of subjects and $\mathcal{I}_s$ the slices of
subject $s$. A reference of $N$ slices is specified by a subject block
$B \subseteq \mathcal{S}$ and a draw from $\bigcup_{s \in B}\mathcal{I}_s$. We
distinguish three protocols:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*, topsep=2pt, itemsep=2pt]
\item[\textsc{head}] the first $N$ filenames in sorted order. This is the
      protocol a directory listing yields by default. Because BraTS filenames
      are ordered by subject identifier, it selects the lowest-numbered
      subjects and, at $N=500$, spans only $17$ of them.
\item[\textsc{balanced}$(m)$] $m$ subjects sampled uniformly at random, with
      slices drawn round-robin --- one per subject before any second --- and
      shuffled within each subject. This fixes $N$ while varying subject
      diversity, and avoids drawing only low-index slices, which are
      volume-edge positions and anatomically atypical.
\item[\textsc{disjoint}] two \textsc{balanced} blocks with no shared subject,
      used wherever a real-versus-real comparison is required.
\end{description}

Unless stated otherwise, results use \textsc{balanced}$(100)$ for the reference
and \textsc{disjoint} blocks for real-versus-real comparisons. Subject-level
disjointness matters: a real-versus-real null built by splitting slices
\emph{within} subjects measures near-duplicate distance, not cohort distance,
and is optimistically small.

\subsection{Feature extraction}
\label{sec:features}

We embed images with RadioDINO-s16 \citep{perezrad}, a ViT-S/16
pretrained on radiological images by self-distillation, held frozen throughout.
Images are resized to $224\times224$, replicated to three channels, and
normalised with the encoder's training statistics. For each of the $L=12$
transformer blocks we take the CLS token of that block's output, giving twelve
$384$-dimensional representations per image from a single forward pass. Reading
every depth costs no more than reading one, which is what makes the audit in
Section~\ref{sec:res_depth} inexpensive.

We write $\phi_\ell(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$ for the block-$\ell$ embedding.
Features are $L_2$-normalised before any distance computation.

\subsection{Distributional discrepancy}
\label{sec:mmd}

For real features $X = \{\phi_\ell(x_i)\}_{i=1}^{m}$ and generated features
$Y = \{\phi_\ell(y_j)\}_{j=1}^{n}$ we use the unbiased MMD$^2$ estimator
\citep{gretton2012kernel}
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mmd}^2(X,Y) =
  \frac{1}{m(m\!-\!1)}\!\sum_{i \neq i'}\! k(x_i,x_{i'})
+ \frac{1}{n(n\!-\!1)}\!\sum_{j \neq j'}\! k(y_j,y_{j'})
- \frac{2}{mn}\sum_{i,j} k(x_i,y_j),
\label{eq:mmd}
\end{equation}
with a multi-bandwidth Gaussian kernel
$k(u,v) = \frac{1}{5}\sum_{c} \exp\!\big(-\|u-v\|^2 / (2 c\,\sigma^2_{\mathrm{med}})\big)$,
$c \in \{\tfrac14, \tfrac12, 1, 2, 4\}$, where $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{med}}$ is the
median pairwise squared distance on the pooled sample. Averaging over
bandwidths avoids committing to a single kernel scale; we report the
single-bandwidth comparison in Section~\ref{sec:res_depth}. The estimator is
unbiased, so values at different $N$ are comparable in expectation --- which is
precisely why the residual dependence on \emph{which} images form the reference
is worth isolating.

\subsection{An empirical inter-cohort reference distribution}
\label{sec:cohortnull}

The conventional null for Eq.~\eqref{eq:mmd} is
$H_0^{\mathrm{perm}}: P_{\mathrm{real}} = P_{\mathrm{gen}}$, tested by pooling
the two samples, relabelling at random, and recomputing. We report it for
comparison, but it is not the hypothesis of interest: no generator satisfies it,
and rejecting it says nothing about whether a generator is usable.

We therefore define a null from the data itself. Partition $\mathcal{S}$ into
$K$ disjoint blocks $B_1, \dots, B_K$ of $m$ subjects each, and draw $N$ slices
from each by the \textsc{balanced} protocol. The
\emph{cohort-heterogeneity null at cohort size $m$} is the set of pairwise
distances
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{N}_m = \big\{\, \widehat{\mmd}^2(R_a, R_b) \;:\; 1 \le a < b \le K \,\big\},
\label{eq:null}
\end{equation}
where $R_a$ is the draw from block $B_a$. For a generated set $G$ scored
against reference $R_1$, we report the cohort-calibrated statistic
\begin{equation}
z_{\mathrm{cohort}} =
  \frac{\widehat{\mmd}^2(R_1, G) - \operatorname{mean}\mathcal{N}_m}
       {\operatorname{sd}\mathcal{N}_m},
\qquad
p_{\mathrm{cohort}} = \frac{1 + |\{v \in \mathcal{N}_m : v \ge \widehat{\mmd}^2(R_1,G)\}|}
                           {1 + |\mathcal{N}_m|}.
\label{eq:cohortz}
\end{equation}
$\mathcal{N}_m$ depends on $m$, so $z_{\mathrm{cohort}}$ is reported together
with the cohort size that produced it. This is a feature rather than a
nuisance: it states in the metric itself how much resolution a reference of that
size affords.

\paragraph{On confidence intervals}
Under $H_0^{\mathrm{perm}}$ the statistic in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mmd} is a degenerate
$U$-statistic, and the ordinary nonparametric bootstrap is not consistent for
it \citep{arcones1992bootstrap}; an $m$-out-of-$n$ bootstrap or subsampling is
required. We therefore never place a bootstrap interval on a null comparison.
Every interval reported here is computed under the alternative --- real against
generated, where the statistic is non-degenerate and the ordinary bootstrap is
consistent --- using $500$ resamples of each sample independently. Null
behaviour is characterised by the empirical distribution $\mathcal{N}_m$
instead, which requires no asymptotic argument.

\subsection{Diagnostics under audit}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

Each diagnostic below is computed at every block $\ell \in \{1,\dots,12\}$ from
the same cached features, and scored by a figure of merit that does not presume
which depth should win.

\paragraph{Distributional fidelity}
Figure of merit: the permutation $z$-score of $\widehat{\mmd}^2(R, G)$ against a
label-shuffling null, which measures how many null standard deviations separate
the observed discrepancy from chance. We additionally check whether each depth
recovers the a-priori severity ordering
$\text{real} < \text{DDPM} < \text{WDM-3D} < \text{CT}$.

\paragraph{Memorization}
A known fraction $r$ of the generated set is replaced by copies of real images
perturbed with Gaussian jitter of scale $\sigma$. The detector flags a generated
image when its nearest-neighbour distance to the reference falls below the
median within-reference nearest-neighbour distance. Figure of merit: the mean
absolute error between the flagged rate and $r$, over
$r \in \{0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4\}$. Sweeping $\sigma$ matters, because at very
small $\sigma$ the task saturates and every depth succeeds.

\paragraph{Coverage}
The generated set is truncated to simulate mode drop, retaining a fraction
$1-d$ for $d \in \{0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8\}$, and $k$-NN precision/recall
\citep{kynkaanniemi2019pr} is computed with $k=5$. Figure of merit: the
Spearman correlation between $d$ and recall, which must be strongly
\emph{negative} for the diagnostic to be usable --- removing diversity must
reduce measured coverage.

\paragraph{Per-image novelty scoring}
Four rules score a single image against the reference: distance to the
reference centroid; $1$-NN and $5$-NN distance; and a Mahalanobis distance using
a covariance estimate shrunk toward a scaled identity
($\hat\Sigma_\varepsilon = (1-\varepsilon)\hat\Sigma + \varepsilon\,\bar\lambda I$,
$\varepsilon = 0.1$), which is necessary because a $384$-dimensional covariance
estimated from $500$ samples is ill-conditioned. Figure of merit: ROC-AUC
separating held-out real images from a positive class. We report two positive
classes of unequal difficulty --- cross-modality CT and the DDPM's own output ---
because a task that saturates cannot distinguish rules or depths.

\paragraph{Degradation monotonicity}
Gaussian noise of increasing $\sigma$ is applied to the generated set. Figure of
merit: Spearman correlation between $\sigma$ and $\widehat{\mmd}^2$, which
should be $+1$.

\subsection{Baselines and implementation}
\label{sec:baselines}

FID and KID are computed with \texttt{torchmetrics} $1.9.0$ at its default
$2048$-dimensional InceptionV3 pool3 features, from \texttt{uint8} inputs at
$256\times256$, letting the library perform its own resize and normalisation.
We flag a pitfall we encountered: a second, hand-rolled FID built on
\texttt{torchvision}'s ImageNet-weight InceptionV3 with ImageNet normalisation
produces values on a different scale for the same images, and mixing the two
within one study yields tables that disagree by tens of FID points while
appearing to describe the same comparison. All FID and KID numbers in this paper
come from one implementation. CMMD \citep{jayasumana2024cmmd} uses CLIP
ViT-L/14 features with the authors' unbiased Gaussian MMD estimator.

Experiments run on a single NVIDIA RTX 4070 Laptop GPU. Extracting all twelve
depths for $500$ images takes $2.5$\,s; one MMD$^2$ evaluation at $N=500$ takes
$4.5$\,s including feature extraction. Building a cohort null from $24$ blocks
requires $24$ extractions and $276$ MMD evaluations. All experiments use seed
$42$ unless a seed sweep is reported.
